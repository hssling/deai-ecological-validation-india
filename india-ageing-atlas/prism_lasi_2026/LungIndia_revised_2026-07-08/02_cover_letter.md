**Re: Original article — "How Much Preserved Ratio Impaired Spirometry in Older Indians Is Real? National Prevalence, Metabolic and Ageing Correlates, and Dependence on the Reference Equation in the Longitudinal Ageing Study in India"**

Dear Editor,

I am pleased to submit the above original article for consideration in *Lung India*. This is a substantially rebuilt version of an earlier analysis of preserved ratio impaired spirometry (PRISm) in the Longitudinal Ageing Study in India (LASI). Expert review of that earlier version rightly argued that a single reference equation and a single spirometric threshold could not support a firm national prevalence claim. I have taken that critique seriously and reconstructed the study around it. A point-by-point response accompanies this submission.

The manuscript now makes an honest, two-part contribution that I believe is of direct interest to Indian chest physicians:

1. **A genuinely common, clinically meaningful pattern.** In the first nationally representative, spirometry-based analysis of older Indians (n = 30,996), a low-volume, non-obstructive pattern is common and independently marks frailty, low handgrip strength, functional limitation, and multimorbidity. It is metabolically patterned—tracking underweight, obesity, and diabetes—rather than smoking-related, mirroring India's nutrition transition.

2. **A prevalence that depends on the reference equation.** By classifying the *same* participants under five reference sets—GLI-2012 (the South-East Asian module I show is embedded in the released LASI data), GLI-Global 2022 race-neutral, the Chhabra-2014 and Agarwal-2020 Indian equations, and an internal LASI healthy-subset reference—PRISm prevalence ranges from 46% to 4%. The recently recommended race-neutral equations do not resolve this; they over-call impairment further, because South Asians sit below even the race-neutral average. Only India-derived references bring the estimate into a plausible range.

The practical message is therefore not a headline count of affected elders, but a call Indian pulmonology is well placed to answer: derive and adopt nationally representative reference equations before the burden of restrictive and preserved-ratio impairment can be stated with confidence. I have deliberately avoided converting a reference-dependent percentage into a national disease count.

This manuscript is original, has not been published previously, and is not under consideration elsewhere. It is the work of a single author. There are no conflicts of interest and no funding to declare. The analysis used de-identified public data and required no separate ethics approval.
