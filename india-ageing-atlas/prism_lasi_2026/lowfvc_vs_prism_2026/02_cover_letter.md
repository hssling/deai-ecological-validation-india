**Re: Original article — "Which Spirometric Flag Better Identifies Frailty and Disability in Older Indians? A Head-to-Head Comparison of GLI-Labelled PRISm and a Correctly-Referenced Low FVC"**

Dear Editor,

I submit the above original article for consideration.

Preserved ratio impaired spirometry (PRISm), defined against the GLI equations, is increasingly promoted as a marker of systemic ageing. Because GLI over-calls impairment in South Asians, the label is applied to roughly two-fifths of older Indians—too many to be specific for anything. In 30,995 adults from the Longitudinal Ageing Study in India, I compared GLI-PRISm head-to-head with a low FVC referenced to a nationally representative Indian standard, judging each not by association alone but by how much risk it concentrates and whether it adds anything beyond age and sex.

The results are clarifying and, I think, honest. GLI-PRISm flags 40% of adults at essentially average risk; a correctly-referenced low FVC flags far fewer (7–16%) at higher relative risk, with stronger adjusted associations—most clearly for low grip strength. Yet no spirometric flag adds meaningful discrimination over demographics for any outcome. The message is therefore twofold: reference spirometry appropriately (the correctly-referenced low FVC is the better flag), but use it modestly (it is not a strong stand-alone marker of ageing morbidity). Because the data are cross-sectional, I am careful to frame this as concurrent identification and to reserve prognostic claims for future waves.

The work is original, single-authored, unfunded, without conflicts, uses de-identified public data, and follows standard reporting. It complements companion analyses from the same programme on reference equations and preserved-ratio impairment.
